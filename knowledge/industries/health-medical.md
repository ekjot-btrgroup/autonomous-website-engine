# Health and medical (focus: medical aesthetics and injector-led med spas)
Covers clinics, practices and med spas. All need the same backbone: who treats you, their qualifications, what
happens at a visit, cost, and how to book. Aesthetics notes cover injector-led practices (toxin, filler, PRF).

Visitors:
- First-timers (largest group): curious but nervous about looking "done", pain, bruising and cost. They want
  to know who will hold the needle, whether results look natural, and what a first visit involves.
- Returning patients: want to rebook fast, see prices, check opening hours, and find their injector.
- Switchers: unhappy with a past result; they look for credentials and an injector who says no when needed.
- What makes them act: a real face, a calm "what to expect" story, visible prices, a low-pressure first step.
Main action: book a consultation (online booking link, one label site-wide, e.g. "Book a consultation").
Secondary: "View treatments and prices". Returning patients get a quiet "Rebook" or "Book a treatment" in the nav.

## Must-have sections (in a sensible order)
- hero: one plain line on the outcome and philosophy (looking like yourself, rested, refreshed), who leads the
  practice (name and credential), location, and the consultation CTA. A real photo of the injector or the room.
- trust strip: licence type, medical oversight, years in practice only if given, product brands used.
- injector / founder: portrait, credentials (RN, NP, PA, MD and state), training, a short first-person story
  about why they practise and how they work (less is more, natural movement, will advise against treatment).
- treatments grouped by goal or category, not a long list: wrinkle relaxers (neuromodulators), dermal fillers,
  biostimulators (e.g. Sculptra), regenerative (PRF), skin. Each card: what it does, typical timing, from-price,
  link to detail. Avoid repeating the same card four times; vary scale or use ruled rows.
- consultation process / what to expect: 3 to 4 steps (consult and facial assessment, plan and consent,
  treatment, follow-up or review appointment). State if the consult fee is credited to treatment, only if true.
- pricing transparency: "from" prices or a per-unit / per-syringe / per-session menu, plus what changes the
  price. Note any consultation fee. If the client will not share prices, say "priced at consultation" plainly.
- real reviews: 3 short quotes from Google or the booking platform, with first name or initial and treatment
  only, plus a link to the source. Never invent or edit them.
- FAQ: does it hurt, bruising and swelling, downtime, when results show (neuromodulator about 2 weeks,
  biostimulators build over weeks to months), how long results last, what to avoid before and after,
  events timing (book well ahead of weddings), who is not a candidate, payment and cancellation.
- booking and contact: booking button, phone, email, hours, and a short form only if the client wants one.
- location: address, map, parking or transit, photo of the entrance or room, accessibility note.
- footer: practice legal name, medical director, licence details, privacy notice, results-vary note.

## Nice to have
- before and after gallery, only with real, consented, unedited photos (see Content checklist).
- membership or loyalty plan if the practice has one; keep it below treatments, never in the hero.
- "Is this right for me?" short guide by concern (lines, volume loss, skin quality) linking to treatments.
- injector-written journal posts; aftercare link; gift cards; virtual consult; second location block.
- general medical practices: insurance accepted, new-patient forms, patient portal link, emergency notice.

## Content checklist
- Trust signals to ask the client for: injector licence type, number and state; medical director name and
  credential (many US states require physician oversight); training and memberships (only real ones);
  product brands actually stocked; real photos of the injector, team and treatment room; review links.
- Facts to ask for: treatments offered, prices or price policy, consult fee, hours, address, booking platform
  URL, years in practice, any awards (only verifiable), patient photo consents on file.
- No guaranteed results. Use "may", "can", "typically", "results vary by person". No "flawless", "ageless",
  "painless", "permanent" or "no downtime" unless literally true for that treatment.
- No unverifiable superlatives: "best", "#1", "top injector", "leading" are out unless an independent,
  citable source says so. "Board-certified" only with the actual board named.
- Brand names used accurately: Botox Cosmetic, Dysport, Xeomin, Jeuveau, Daxxify and Letybo are distinct
  products. Only name the brands the practice actually uses; never call another brand "Botox"; use
  "neuromodulator" or "wrinkle relaxer" as the generic term. Same for fillers (Juvederm, Restylane, RHA) and
  Sculptra or Radiesse. Do not claim "FDA-approved" for an area or use unless it is an approved indication;
  PRF is made from the patient's own blood and is not a branded or approved drug, so describe it plainly.
- UK clients: prescription-only medicines (botulinum toxin) may not be advertised to the public. Do not name the
  product or brand on UK sites; ASA rulings show even indirect wording and per-area toxin prices can breach it.
  Lead with "consultation" and describe concerns, not the drug.
- Before and after: separate written marketing consent per patient (treatment consent is not photo consent),
  no editing, same lighting, angle and expression, treatment and timing in the caption, a "results vary" note,
  no faces cropped to look worse. Never use photos from brands, other clinics or stock as patient results.
- Testimonials: real only (FTC rule since Oct 2024 bans fake, bought or AI-written reviews and testimonials).
  Show a patient's name, photo or treatment details only with written HIPAA-style authorisation; otherwise use
  initials. Never reply to reviews with patient details. No incentives for positive reviews.
- Forms: ask the minimum (name, contact, interest); keep medical history for the secure intake system.
- Placeholders must be clearly marked: [Injector name, RN], [Price from], [Medical director], [Review source].

## Look and feel
- Mood: luxurious but warm, a calm private appointment, not a hospital. Quiet confidence over glamour.
- Photo style: real injector portraits with natural light, hands at work, the treatment room, texture close-ups
  (linen, stone, glass, botanicals). Faces with natural skin texture and movement, not frozen or retouched.
  Inclusive casting across skin tones, ages (30s to 60s) and genders, since men and darker skin tones are often
  missing; light and grade every skin tone well.
- Colour moods: bone, ivory, sand and stone neutrals with one warm accent (clay, cocoa, champagne, blush,
  muted rose) or a deep anchor (espresso, moss, oxblood) used sparingly; or a strict black and white UI that
  lets warm photography carry colour. Avoid saturated medical blue and pure clinical white-on-white.
- Type moods: a refined serif (high-contrast or condensed) for headlines with an italic phrase for warmth,
  paired with a clean grotesk for body, labels and prices; or one grotesk with tight tracking for a modern
  clinic. Small caps labels and a soft accent dot read precise without feeling cold.
- Premium level: high. Generous white space, few sections done well, prices set calmly in a menu, not a promo.
- Motion: low to medium. Slow hero image settle, gentle fade-and-rise reveals, word-by-word headline reveal at
  most once. No tickers of discount offers, no bouncy effects. Respect reduced motion.
- What the best references share (curevanta, lustre, stillhour, wellges, wellnesse, essential):
  - A restrained, near-neutral UI (ivory, bone, sand, white, ink) where photography supplies the warmth,
    with one deep or warm accent used sparingly.
  - Display serif headlines with one italic phrase (lustre, stillhour, wellges) or a single tight grotesk at
    one weight (curevanta, wellnesse); hierarchy from size, never from many weights.
  - Large inset rounded image cards (8 to 12px page gutter, 24 to 32px radius) and pill buttons; soft glass
    captions over photos instead of heavy overlays.
  - A recurring section header (eyebrow pill plus heading left, short copy and button right).
  - Practitioner portraits, a numbered "how it works" step sequence, an accordion FAQ beside a help card,
    and a calm, mostly static page with slow reveals.
  - Their shared weaknesses to fix: too many near-identical card blocks, stock spa imagery, and placeholder
    stats and ratings. Keep it shorter and use only real figures.

## Avoid
- Clinical blue and white stock, stethoscopes, syringes pointed at camera, gloved hands in close-up.
- Overfilled-face stock: frozen foreheads, overdone lips, airbrushed plastic skin, models who all look alike.
- Discount-coupon banners, "$X off" pop-ups, countdown timers, "Botox party" language, flash-sale tickers.
- Shock before/after grids, red-circled flaws, extreme or unconsented results, a gallery as the hero.
- Fake or AI reviews, invented star ratings, "500+ happy clients" numbers the client did not supply.
- Superlatives and guarantees ("best injector in town", "permanent", "risk-free"); calling every
  neuromodulator "Botox"; logos of products the practice does not stock.
- Hiding the injector: an anonymous brand with no name, face or licence is the biggest trust killer here.
- Long treatment menus with 30 equal tiles; jargon-heavy copy; booking hidden below the fold or behind a form.
